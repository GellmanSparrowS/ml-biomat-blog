How Long Chains Crystallize: Structure, Growth and Properties

Start with a thin polyethylene crystal and connect the scales of lattices, lamellae and spherulites. Nucleation costs, molecular mobility, transformation rates and melting enthalpy explain how cooling, nucleating agents, flow and annealing change clarity, dimensions and deformation.

Why the same long chains produce different solids

Cooling the same plastic rapidly or slowly can produce different stiffness, transparency and shrinkage. Drawing a film can make it stronger in a preferred direction. The chemical composition need not change: some chain segments develop regular crystalline arrangements, other segments remain less ordered, and both become organized at larger scales. Polymer crystallization concerns how long chains establish this organization during the limited time available in manufacturing, and how the resulting structures influence the finished material.

A salt crystal suggests neatly packed small particles, while a polymer resembles a very long cord. A short section can align with neighboring sections, but it remains attached to the rest of its molecule and entangled with other chains. Polymers therefore commonly form semicrystalline materials containing both crystalline and amorphous regions. One molecule may enter a crystal, pass through less ordered material and enter another crystal. Crystallinity describes how much material is assigned to the crystalline state. It does not specify crystal thickness, overall size, orientation or connectivity.

Structural hierarchy is the first useful distinction. A unit cell describes periodic atomic or segmental arrangement at the smallest scale. A lamella is a thin crystal containing many ordered chain sections. A spherulite is a larger organization of lamellae and intervening amorphous material growing outward from a center. The distinction resembles rooms, buildings and neighborhoods: the levels are related but not interchangeable. A circular spherulite observed under a microscope is not a single uninterrupted crystal, and its diameter is not the length of one polymer molecule.

Work by Keller, Till, Fischer and others in 1957 advanced the study of thin polyethylene single crystals. Electron diffraction showed chain axes oriented approximately along the thin dimension of the lamella, although the contour length of a complete chain was much greater than the lamellar thickness. Keller connected these observations through chain folding: a chain could enter the crystal, turn near a surface and return as another ordered stem. The argument joined crystal shape, diffraction direction and molecular length, establishing a distinctive structural feature of polymer crystals.

Folds in real materials need not resemble perfectly regular neighboring turns in a textbook drawing. Chains can form longer loops, leave one lamella and enter another as tie molecules, or retain ends and entanglements in amorphous regions. Regular platelets grown from dilute solution also differ from lamellar organizations formed in a concentrated melt. Chain folding identifies an important geometric constraint, while strength and toughness depend additionally on tie molecules, crystal slip and deformation of surrounding amorphous chains. A complete mechanical interpretation must include the connections between ordered regions.

Lattices describe positions; unit cells describe repetition

A tiled floor provides a simple analogy. Repeating a pattern by translation in two directions covers a plane; a three-dimensional periodic crystal repeats in three directions. The lattice is the mathematical array of equivalent positions, and the motif is the atomic or molecular structure placed at each position. Together they specify a crystal structure. A unit cell is a region used to describe the repetition. It need not have the smallest possible volume or be cubic: a conventional cell is often selected to display symmetry more clearly.

Three edge lengths and three angles describe a unit-cell shape, while symmetry includes rotations, reflections, inversion and combinations with translation. A screw axis combines rotation with an advance along an axis; a glide plane combines reflection with translation. Ordinary three-dimensional periodic crystals have seven crystal systems and fourteen Bravais lattices. Combining internal arrangements with spatial symmetry gives the richer classification of space groups. For a beginner, the useful task is understanding which structural rule each concept describes, rather than treating the number of categories as a table of material properties.

Translational periodicity restricts rotational symmetry, allowing one-, two-, three-, four- and sixfold rotations in ordinary three-dimensional lattices. This restriction concerns periodic arrangements. Quasicrystals demonstrate that ordered structures can lack ordinary three-dimensional translational periodicity while producing sharp diffraction peaks. Consequently, neither all crystals must be periodically repeated nor every object with a local fivefold pattern is a quasicrystal provides an adequate general definition. Periodic unit cells nevertheless remain a highly useful starting point for the common crystalline forms of polymers such as PE and PP.

Crystal planes can be labeled with three integers called Miller indices. Their construction starts from the intercepts of parallel planes with the unit-cell axes and forms ratios from reciprocal intercepts. A plane parallel to an axis gives zero for that direction. Indices in round brackets describe planes, while square brackets commonly describe directions. Identical-looking numbers do not make the two objects equivalent. Growth faces, diffraction peaks and orientation analysis repeatedly use these labels because they turn geometric relationships into names that can be compared between experiments.

X-ray wavelengths are comparable to atomic spacings. Waves scattered from ordered regions reinforce one another in particular directions, creating diffraction peaks. Bragg’s relation expresses this condition through the path difference between waves associated with neighboring planes. The angle needs careful identification: the equation uses the angle between the incident beam and a plane, while a conventional diffraction plot often uses the scattering angle between the beams, twice as large. Confusing these angles produces an incorrect interplanar spacing even when the numerical calculation itself is performed correctly.

Peak positions help identify spacings and crystal forms. Width, shape and angular distribution provide additional information. Small crystalline domains, defects, internal strain and instrumental resolution can all broaden peaks. Strong orientation concentrates the intensity of an otherwise nearly complete diffraction ring into arcs or spots. A sharp peak therefore supports the presence of ordered material, but its height alone is not a measure of total crystalline content. Background, orientation and integration range must also be considered when estimating crystallinity.

Real crystals depart from an ideal lattice. Vacancies, foreign units, chain ends and irregular folds can disturb local packing. Dislocations introduce mismatches around a line, while boundaries join regions with different orientations. Such defects influence growth, slip and failure, but a boundary does not invariably stop a crack. In polymers, imperfections are also entangled with molecular connectivity: some local disorder is necessary for chains to connect different crystals.

Nucleation and growth compete during cooling

Below the relevant equilibrium melting temperature, forming a crystal can lower the bulk free energy. A very small ordered cluster must also create an interface between crystal and surrounding melt, which carries an energetic cost. Free energy provides a measure of the preferred direction of change at specified temperature and pressure, incorporating interactions and available arrangements. A tiny cluster has much surface relative to its volume, so interfacial cost can dominate and the cluster disappears. Beyond a critical size, the volume benefit increases more rapidly, making further growth favorable.

The simplest illustration assumes a spherical nucleus. Its surface cost increases with radius squared, while its bulk benefit increases with radius cubed. Subtracting the latter from the former produces a free-energy curve that first rises and then falls. The maximum identifies a critical radius. Actual polymer nuclei have anisotropic shapes and different surfaces, so they need not be spherical. The purpose of this model is to expose competition between area and volume, rather than to obtain the actual shape of every polymer nucleus from one universal formula.

Homogeneous nucleation occurs within the material without a specified foreign surface. Heterogeneous nucleation uses a particle, container wall, residual ordered structure or another surface to change the interfacial cost. An effective nucleating agent does not simply make every material colder; it provides favorable conditions for particular crystalline arrangements. Growth at an existing crystal surface can also require formation of a new ordered layer, commonly described through secondary nucleation. Primary nucleation establishes where a crystal begins, while processes at its surface determine how it continues to advance.

Greater undercooling generally increases the thermodynamic drive to crystallize, but chains must still move into the correct arrangements. At sufficiently low temperatures, approaching a region of strongly restricted glassy motion, rearrangement becomes difficult and growth may slow substantially. Near equilibrium melting, segments move readily but the drive to form stable nuclei and growth layers is small. Crystallization therefore commonly proceeds fastest within an intermediate temperature range. It does not follow a simple colder-means-faster rule across the entire interval.

Cooling rate changes the time spent in each temperature range. Rapid cooling can retain substantial amorphous material, or in particular systems produce many small crystals or mesomorphic structures. Slower cooling generally allows more time for growth and reorganization. The result depends on molecular mobility, nucleation conditions and the actual temperature history. Cooling rate alone cannot guarantee that toughness rises or falls, because defects, tie molecules, crystal form, orientation and residual stress can all change simultaneously. The processing variable must be connected to the structures it actually creates.

Flow supplies another way to organize chains. Shear or extension can align parts of molecules, reducing the rearrangement needed to form particular ordered structures. Longer oriented segments may also support subsequent crystal growth. Once flow stops, orientation relaxes with time, so deformation intensity, duration and subsequent cooling must be considered jointly. Flow-induced nucleation is not determined by a machine’s rotational speed alone. Its effect depends strongly on chain-length distribution and the time over which the material retains its molecular orientation.

The heating history used to prepare a melt affects its next crystallization. Insufficient temperature or holding time can leave residual crystals, stable order or orientation memory that advances subsequent crystallization. Excessively severe heating can instead degrade chains. Comparing nucleating agents therefore requires a consistent, suitable melting program for both the control and modified samples. Differences in their starting states should not be attributed entirely to the additive.

Separating crystallization speed from final quantity

Bringing a sample rapidly to a selected temperature and holding it there allows heat release, diffraction or volume changes to be followed over time. A slow beginning, rapid middle stage and gradually approaching plateau form a common curve. Its vertical axis requires definition. Relative transformation treats the final crystallization change observed in that experiment as completion, while absolute crystallinity describes the crystalline proportion of the material. Reaching 100% relative transformation therefore does not mean that every chain segment has entered a crystal.

Avrami’s phase-transformation theory, developed from 1939 onward, connected nucleation, growth and competition for space. One can first imagine each region expanding according to its growth rule, then correct for volumes counted more than once. This accounts for transformation slowing as regions meet. Real crystals are not assumed to pass through one another and grow independently forever. Applied to polymers, the relation can describe a useful conversion range, while later lamellar thickening, secondary crystallization or changes in nucleation behavior can produce departures from the initial description.

The following form uses the time needed to reach half of the relative transformation as a rate measure, together with an exponent controlling curve shape. A shorter half-time moves the overall change earlier. The exponent changes the early rise and the steepness of the middle section. This form avoids directly comparing rate coefficients whose units change with exponent. Interpreting the exponent physically still requires assumptions about nucleation and growth. A fitted number cannot be translated into one unique microscopic mechanism without other observations.

For example, ideal three-dimensional growth at constant speed with all nuclei established initially can give an exponent of 3. Constant formation of new nuclei during such growth can give 4. Confined growth in a film, time-dependent nucleation, changing growth rates and overlapping processes can produce noninteger exponents or similar fitted values from different mechanisms. A mechanistic interpretation should combine the curve with microscopy showing when nuclei appear, how crystal radii develop and which dimensions are constrained by the specimen geometry.

An isothermal curve also cannot be used directly as an injection-molding cooling curve. During cooling, nucleation and growth rates vary continuously, and different material layers experience different shear and pressure histories. Isothermal experiments can first separate differences between formulations, followed by heating and cooling programs closer to manufacturing. Before fitting, the time origin, the delay in establishing temperature and the final change used for normalization must be checked. Otherwise, differences introduced by the instrument or data treatment can be mistaken for differences in the crystallization mechanism.

Lamellar thickness, spherulite size and connecting chains

Although lamellae are thin, their thickness has a substantial effect on melting. Upper and lower surfaces contribute more strongly per unit volume in a thin platelet, making it less stable than an ideal infinitely thick crystal. The Gibbs–Thomson relation expresses how melting temperature approaches the equilibrium value as thickness increases. Surface free energy, fusion enthalpy per unit volume and thickness require compatible units. Entering nanometers as meters, or using enthalpy per gram where energy per cubic meter is required, destroys the quantitative meaning of the calculation.

A higher crystallization temperature often favors thicker lamellae. Annealing can also permit thickening or reorganization, provided sufficient time and mobility are available. During a heating scan, thin crystals can melt first, after which chains reorganize and melt again at a higher temperature. Two melting peaks therefore do not necessarily establish two chemical components or even two crystal forms. Different heating rates, annealing experiments and diffraction help separate structures present before measurement from changes occurring during the measurement itself.

Spherulite size instead depends on the number of nuclei and the space available for growth. With few nuclei, each organization can expand farther. With many nuclei, neighboring organizations meet earlier and remain smaller. Growth stops in directions blocked by neighboring material, so final outlines cease to be complete spheres. Two samples with similar crystalline mass can therefore contain a few large spherulites or many small ones. The distinction changes light scattering, local deformation and the distribution of boundaries through the material.

The dark cross often observed under crossed polarizers arises from anisotropic structure interacting with polarized light. Approximately radial lamellar orientations make particular directions appear dark for the selected optical arrangement. The pattern indicates radial organization, rather than four discrete crystals inside one spherulite. Alternating light and dark rings can involve changes in lamellar orientation and twisting, with the detailed interpretation depending on material and imaging conditions. Polarized microscopy records an optical response; electron microscopy supplies complementary information about the physical lamellar organization responsible for it.

Transparency depends on the size, magnitude and spatial correlations of refractive-index variations. Refining spherulites or other scattering structures can improve clarity, but a diameter crossing one visible wavelength is not a universal opacity threshold. Part thickness, orientation, voids and additives also matter. Mechanical behavior likewise requires the connecting chains. More or thicker crystals may increase stiffness in certain conditions without universally reducing toughness. Crystal slip, reorganization, cavitation and chain stretching near a crack determine how deformation energy is dissipated.

Crystallization commonly involves volume contraction. When different regions crystallize at different times, they constrain one another’s dimensional changes. A surface that cools before the interior contracts can develop residual stress, sink marks or warpage; oriented structures can shrink anisotropically. Faster crystallization may shorten the wait before demolding, while changing how much crystallization occurs during packing, before release or afterward. Processing therefore needs the location and timing of crystallization as well as its final amount.

Cross-checking calorimetry, diffraction, density and spectroscopy

Differential scanning calorimetry, or DSC, compares the heat flow required by a sample and a reference during a programmed temperature history. Crystal melting absorbs heat, while crystallization generally releases it. Different instruments can plot endothermic heat flow upward or downward, so the axis convention must be checked first. Integrating a melting peak relative to a baseline gives energy, and dividing by sample mass gives fusion enthalpy per gram. Baseline selection, overlapping processes and scan rate all affect the resulting area.

If cold crystallization occurs during heating, the subsequent melting enthalpy includes crystals formed during the scan. To estimate crystallinity present beforehand, a common procedure subtracts the cold-crystallization enthalpy under suitable conditions and compares the difference with a fully crystalline reference. If the sample contains glass fibers or another component that does not contribute to melting of the polymer being measured, polymer mass fraction must also enter the denominator. Otherwise, increasing reinforcement can create an artificial decline in the reported polymer crystallinity.

The reference fusion enthalpy must match the chemistry and crystal form. Different reference values, histories and normalization conventions can produce systematic differences between published crystallinities. A first heating scan primarily retains information about the specimen’s earlier history. Applying a common cooling program before a second heating helps compare materials after a more consistent treatment. Both measurements are useful, but the second heating should not automatically be interpreted as the original product structure. The measurement sequence is part of the definition of the result.

X-ray diffraction distinguishes ordered crystalline contributions from broader amorphous scattering. Estimating crystallinity requires a reasonable background, sufficient angular coverage and attention to orientation and peak overlap. Density methods instead use the difference between crystalline and amorphous densities within a two-phase model. Mass fractions relate to specific volume; a volume fraction obtained by linear density averaging cannot simply be relabeled as a mass fraction. Pores, fillers, copolymer composition and measurement temperature all affect the interpretation.

Infrared spectroscopy measures vibrational absorption. Some bands respond to chain conformation or crystalline environment, and comparing them with suitable reference bands can track structural change. Thickness, orientation, composition and absorption coefficients also affect intensity. A stronger band can reflect more of a particular conformation or a more favorable orientation for absorbing the incident light. Absolute crystallinity therefore needs a calibrated relationship. Calorimetry probes energy changes, diffraction probes spatial order and spectroscopy probes local environments; agreement among them supports a more complete interpretation.

When methods give different numbers, first compare their operational definitions of crystalline material and their sample conditions. Small, defective ordered regions may be assigned differently by thermal and diffraction analyses. Continued crystallization during a scan introduces another difference. Clearly recording mass, filler content, temperature history and integration choices is usually more informative than selecting one supposedly correct crystallinity without examining the definitions. The purpose is to explain the material’s structure, including the features that individual measurements emphasize differently.

Amorphous segments close to crystal surfaces can be more strongly constrained than amorphous material farther away. A crystalline–amorphous two-phase model is a convenient starting point, while more detailed descriptions distinguish a constrained amorphous population. It can affect heat-capacity changes through the glass transition and mechanical relaxation without creating a distinct crystalline diffraction peak. Materials with the same nominal crystallinity can consequently differ in segmental mobility and compliance.

PE and PP: different crystals built from related principles

Polyethylene has a relatively simple backbone. Segments in common crystals can adopt approximately planar zigzag conformations within an orthorhombic lattice. PE single crystals grown from dilute solution can have diamond-shaped, truncated or more complex outlines, reflecting different growth rates of their faces under particular solution and temperature conditions. A regular outline does not establish equal perfection everywhere: defects and thickness variation can remain inside the lamella. Transport limitations and interfacial instability can also produce branched, dendritic forms.

PE crystallizing from a melt commonly develops lamellar and spherulitic structures. Branches affect the length and placement of sequences that enter crystals; molecular-length distribution influences mobility and connectivity; cooling and annealing alter lamellar thickness. Special conditions such as high pressure can create extended-chain structures different from ordinary folded lamellae. Strong flow can also produce shish-kebab organizations, with an oriented axial structure resembling a skewer and transverse lamellae resembling disks along it. The analogy describes morphology; it does not establish that every axial structure consists of fully extended molecules of identical length.

Regular methyl-group placement in isotactic PP allows a helical chain conformation in common crystals. Similar helices can pack differently to form α, β and γ polymorphs, as well as mesomorphic structures with different degrees of order. Crystal form and molecular configuration belong to different structural levels. Converting between α and β arrangements does not turn an isotactic chain into an atactic one. Nucleating agents, pressure, flow, sequence defects and temperature history can all influence which arrangements develop.

The common α form is monoclinic. The β form has a different packing arrangement and can favor substantial plastic deformation in particular formulations and loading conditions. The γ form is described by an orthorhombic structure and can contain neighboring chain layers whose axes point in different directions. Meille and Brückner’s 1989 work on γ-PP showed that polymer crystals can accommodate nonparallel chains in an ordered arrangement. Parallel chains are therefore a common structural pattern, rather than a rule required of every polymer crystal.

Deformation of β-rich PP can involve crystal reorganization, cavitation and phase transformation, while final impact behavior also depends on phase proportion, lamellar organization, orientation and test conditions. One crystallization temperature cannot automatically prescribe α or β formation in every PP grade. Nor should one polymorph be universally labeled tough and another brittle. After changing a nucleating system, crystal-form composition, structural size and the deformation process should all be examined to establish which structural change produced the observed improvement.

Applying crystallization principles to PET, nylon, PTFE and PLA

PET’s aromatic units and connections can prevent complete crystallization within some processing cooling cycles. A rapidly cooled, relatively amorphous sample can cold-crystallize on reheating when sufficient segmental mobility returns. Crystallization while cooling from the melt belongs to a different part of the thermal program. These phenomena do not require every PET sample to show two peaks at fixed temperatures. Stretch blow molding additionally introduces orientation and strain-induced crystallization, producing structures that jointly affect strength, barrier behavior and optical transmission.

Nylons organize through amide groups and hydrogen bonding. Methylene length, connection direction and crystal form change the hydrogen-bond network. Water affects amorphous mobility and local interactions, sometimes promoting reorganization or changing crystal form under suitable conditions. The outcome depends on nylon chemistry, temperature and moisture state. Melt processing must still follow the drying and moisture requirements of the particular resin. A possible increase in crystallization mobility does not make wet feedstock an automatically preferable processing choice.

Poly(tetrafluoroethylene), or PTFE, combines a fluorinated regular chain structure with very high melt viscosity. Common high-molar-mass PTFE remains difficult to flow like an ordinary injection-molding resin even after its crystals have melted, so powder forming and sintering are frequently used. Crystallinity changes with synthesis state, molar mass, sintering and cooling. A nearly fully crystalline value measured for one original powder cannot be assigned to every finished PTFE component. Crystal–crystal structural transitions near room temperature are also distinct from high-temperature crystal melting.

PTFE does not possess just one extended-chain morphology. Different samples can exhibit ribbon-like, folded and other hierarchical organizations, and first melting can differ from the behavior of previously melted material. The same general question reappears: a polymer name identifies possible chemistry, while preparation and thermal history establish the structure actually measured. Comparisons of friction, dimensions and fracture therefore need crystallinity and morphology information from the relevant treatment state. Otherwise, differences in processing can be incorrectly attributed solely to the chemical family.

Poly(lactic acid), or PLA, highlights stereochemical sequence. PLLA and PDLA have regular sequences of opposite handedness and can each crystallize. Randomly combining opposite-handed units usually disrupts long-range regularity, while bringing sufficiently long chains of the two handednesses together can create a new stereocomplex crystal. In 1987, Ikada, Jamshidi, Tsuji and Hyon reported this phenomenon. It showed that mixing two chain populations can create an ordered structure with a higher melting temperature than their ordinary individual crystals, rather than simply disrupting crystallization.

PLA crystallization rates depend on stereochemical purity, molecular length, nucleating agents, temperature and processing history. A renewable feedstock does not by itself establish a unique crystallization mechanism. During hydrolysis, water often accesses amorphous regions more readily, while the crystalline fraction may rise as more reactive material disappears or remaining chains reorganize. Decreasing mass, decreasing chain length and increasing crystallinity can therefore occur together. Coordinating degradation with mechanical retention requires examining transport, bond cleavage, structural evolution and part dimensions as a connected process.

Three questions organize the design of a crystalline polymer: how much ordered structure is needed, how quickly it must form, and how its regions should connect and orient. Nucleating agents change where and under what conditions growth begins. Temperature history controls mobility and stability, flow introduces direction, and subsequent heat treatment modifies the existing organization. Calorimetry, diffraction and microscopy can follow these changes before they are related to clarity, shrinkage and deformation. This approach turns a single crystallinity percentage into a practical description of structure and processing.
